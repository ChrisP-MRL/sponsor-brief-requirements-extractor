TOGAF BDAT Mining-Adapted Enterprise Architecture Framework Mining-industry-specific modifications to the standard BDAT framework

Status: Active for mining organisations Version 2.0 Created 10 August 2026 Last updated 10 August 2026

This rule adapts the TOGAF Business, Data, Application and Technology (BDAT) hierarchy for mining organisations. It preserves BDAT's architectural discipline while addressing geographic distribution, IT/OT convergence, safety-critical operations, distributed governance, and the operational realities of mine sites.

Activation Triggers & Scope

This rule activates when Claude receives queries, requests, or discussions involving mining enterprise architecture. It is deliberately scoped to enterprise-level architecture work, not tactical operations or engineering decisions.

Activates for

  • Mining enterprise architecture design, analysis, or assessment
  • Mining business transformation or digital modernisation initiatives
  • Mining technology strategy or decisions addressing mining-specific constraints
  • Mining data governance or data strategy addressing remote operations and regulatory complexity
  • Mining application portfolio management spanning IT and OT systems
  • IT-business alignment in mining organisations
  • Mining operational technology convergence (IT/OT integration)
  • Risk management related to mining enterprise systems
  • Mining change management addressing field operations culture
  • Mining architecture governance balancing corporate standardisation with site autonomy
  • Mine site digital transformation initiatives

Out of scope

  • Single system or application-only technical decisions, unless part of mining enterprise architecture
  • Tactical mining operations (shift-by-shift production decisions)
  • Vendor product evaluation for specific mining equipment
  • Mine engineering or geological decisions — these inform architecture but do not constitute it

Foundational Principles for Mining BDAT

These six principles distinguish mining-adapted BDAT from generic enterprise architecture. Each carries an explicit commitment that Claude applies when the principle is in play.

1Bidirectional Strategy Flow

Mining business architecture is not unidirectional top-down strategy deployment. Corporate strategy flows down; site-level constraints flow up. Effective mining business architecture integrates corporate strategy with site-level operational realities through iterative planning.

Claude commitment
  • Identify both corporate-level and site-level business drivers
  • Acknowledge site-level operational knowledge as legitimate input to corporate strategy
  • Map where corporate standardisation is required versus where site flexibility applies
  • Define escalation pathways for corporate/site disagreements

2Co-Equal IT/OT Architecture

Mining does not have a single "Technology Architecture" domain. Mining architecture explicitly recognises two:

  • Information Technology (IT): supporting enterprise functions — ERP, analytics, reporting
  • Operational Technology (OT): controlling mine operations — drilling, extraction, processing

These domains have different governance requirements, change management practices, and risk profiles. BDAT architecture must address both with equal rigour.

Claude commitment
  • When discussing technology, explicitly identify IT domain, OT domain, or IT/OT convergence
  • Acknowledge that IT and OT have incompatible philosophies (agility versus reliability)
  • For convergence points, explicitly address cultural and technical integration challenges
  • Preserve OT autonomy for operational technology; do not force OT to adopt IT practices

3Safety and Reliability Before Agility and Innovation

Mining cannot adopt modern enterprise architecture's prioritisation of continuous innovation and rapid iteration. Mining operations are safety-critical; failures can cause worker injury or death. This requires conservative technology choices and extensive validation even when this constrains innovation velocity.

Claude commitment
  • For safety-critical systems, prioritise deterministic behaviour over probabilistic AI
  • Require extensive validation before production deployment of OT systems
  • Flag when innovations create safety risks requiring management decision
  • Balance efficiency improvements against safety risk increases

4Distributed Operations Governance

Mining operates with geographically dispersed sites requiring semi-autonomous decision-making. Architecture governance must balance corporate standardisation with site-level autonomy through federated governance models, not centralised control.

Claude commitment
  • Distinguish corporate-level architecture decisions from site-level decisions
  • Identify corporate standards versus acceptable site-level variations
  • Define federated governance with corporate oversight and site autonomy
  • Acknowledge that one-size-fits-all solutions often fail in mining

5Mining-Specific Environment Constraints

Mining environments impose technology constraints rare in conventional enterprises: remote connectivity, harsh operating conditions, safety-critical equipment, commodity price volatility, and a specialised workforce. Architecture decisions must acknowledge these constraints flowing upward to guide application and data design.

Claude commitment
  • Identify mining environment constraints affecting architecture decisions
  • Map how constraints flow upward from environment to technology to application to data
  • Document bidirectional traceability — downward requirements plus upward constraints
  • Validate feasibility assumptions with mining operational personnel

6Governance Earned Through Demonstrated Value

Mining organisations have experienced technology disappointment. Framework adoption requires proving the framework helps solve recognised operational challenges. Governance processes imposed without demonstrating value will fail.

Claude commitment
  • Connect BDAT governance to specific mining operational challenges
  • Demonstrate how the framework helps address problems field operations have experienced
  • Celebrate early wins and visible operational improvements
  • Adapt governance based on field feedback; remain open to modifications

Mining-Adapted Tier 1: Business Architecture

Definition

Defines business strategy, governance, organisation, and key business processes at both corporate and site levels, with explicit integration between levels.

Relationship rule

Business Architecture must acknowledge both corporate strategy and site-level operational constraints. Neither dominates; both inform architecture.

Key modifications from standard BDAT

1. Dual-level business planning

  • Corporate level: strategic direction, financial targets, ESG commitments, portfolio decisions, enterprise-wide capability standards
  • Site level: local production targets, geological reality, equipment and personnel capacity, site-specific operational excellence initiatives
  • Integration: iterative planning cycles where corporate strategy accommodates site-level realities, and site operations implement corporate strategy with local adaptations within approved parameters

2. Mining business capability reference model

Use The Open Group EMMM (Exploration, Mining, Metals and Minerals) Forum's Business Capability Reference Map as the foundation:

CapabilityCoverage
DiscoverExploration processes
EstablishMine development and permitting
ExploitProduction operations
BeneficiateMineral processing
SellMarket sales and customer relationships
SupportEnvironmental, safety, HR and compliance functions

Tailor this reference model to the specific mining company portfolio and operations.

3. Federated governance structure

  • Corporate governance: sets architectural principles, approved platforms, and standardised processes applying enterprise-wide
  • Regional/site governance: makes site-specific adaptations within corporate principles; selects approved technologies; implements processes adapted to local conditions
  • Escalation pathway: a clear process for site-level decisions requiring corporate approval when site operations cannot meet corporate targets

4. Mining-specific success metrics

Business Architecture defines success across multiple dimensions:

  • Production targets (ore extraction, processing, beneficiation)
  • Cost control (operating within budget despite commodity volatility)
  • Safety performance (LTI rates, near-miss tracking)
  • Environmental compliance (regulatory adherence, ESG targets)
  • Equipment reliability (uptime, MTBF)
  • Workforce retention (addressing labour market constraints)

Success requires achievement across all dimensions, not maximising any single metric at the expense of others.

Claude responsibility
  • Establish business architecture at the appropriate scope (corporate, regional, or site)
  • Document both corporate strategy and site-level operational realities
  • Identify where corporate standardisation is required versus site flexibility
  • Map how site-level constraints and knowledge inform corporate strategy
  • Reference the EMMM Business Capability Reference Model for mining-specific capabilities
  • Define success metrics across mining-specific dimensions
  • Identify a governance structure balancing corporate oversight with site autonomy
Validation question for Claude
"What business strategy or capability does this architecture serve — at corporate level, at site level, or at both with integration? What site-level operational constraints inform corporate strategy?"

If this cannot be answered clearly, signal that Business Architecture clarification is needed at both levels before proceeding.

Mining-Adapted Tier 2A: Data Architecture

Definition

Models, regulations, rules, and standards determining which data will be gathered, stored, structured, integrated, and used. Explicitly addresses mining's unique data challenges: remote collection with intermittent connectivity, regulatory complexity across jurisdictions, and siloed operations.

Relationship rule

Data Architecture must directly map to and support Business Architecture requirements while addressing mining-specific data collection and governance challenges.

1. Mining data domains

Structure data architecture around mining business domains:

  • Exploration data: geological surveys, assay results, prospect mapping, resource estimation. Sparse point data, scientifically intensive, strategically sensitive.
  • Production operations data: real-time sensor data from drilling, blasting, loading, transportation. High-velocity continuous data from remote sites with intermittent connectivity. Requires edge curation before transmission.
  • Processing data: mineral processing parameters, throughput, grade recovery, waste generation. Reflects process optimisation opportunities; requires integration across processing stations.
  • Environmental and safety data: air quality, water usage, waste, incident reports, near-miss tracking. Supports regulatory compliance; spans multiple external regulatory frameworks varying by jurisdiction.
  • Supply chain and logistics data: ore routing, energy consumption, equipment and personnel logistics. Enables supply chain optimisation; requires coordination across geographically dispersed operations.

2. Mining data governance addressing specific challenges

Challenge A: Remote site data collection with limited connectivity

Problem

Mining generates massive data volumes at remote sites with unreliable connectivity. Traditional data governance assumes centralised repositories. Mining's reality differs fundamentally.

Mining-adapted solution — layered architecture
  • Edge data curation: data filtered, validated and pre-processed at the mine site to reduce transmission volume
  • Layered storage: hot data (immediate needs) at site; warm data (recent history) in regional centres; cold data (archive) in the enterprise repository
  • Asynchronous synchronisation: data synchronises during connectivity windows rather than requiring continuous real-time access

This architecture acknowledges mining's connectivity constraints while maintaining data consistency and traceability.

Challenge B: Data ownership ambiguity in siloed operations

Problem

Mining data spans ownership boundaries — exploration to drilling to production to beneficiation. Each function maintains separate systems, resulting in unclear data stewardship, quality gaps, and siloed analytics.

Mining-adapted solution — three-tier stewardship model
  • Data producer stewardship: the function generating data maintains initial quality, validation and documentation (for example, drilling operations)
  • Data consumer stewardship: functions using data validate fitness-for-purpose and flag quality issues (for example, production planning)
  • Enterprise data stewardship: corporate governance ensures cross-domain consistency, enforces regulatory compliance, and maintains lineage

This model acknowledges shared responsibility; data quality is a collective effort.

Challenge C: Regulatory complexity across jurisdictions

Problem

Mining operates across multiple jurisdictions with conflicting requirements — 10-year versus 25-year retention, conflicting data collection rules, and different tax standards.

Mining-adapted solution — regulatory compliance mapping
  • Regulatory data mapping: document which data elements support which regulatory requirements in which jurisdictions
  • Retention policy management: track regulatory minimums and maximums by jurisdiction
  • Compliance audit trails: data lineage and transformation tracking enables regulatory verification

This architecture enables compliance across a complex regulatory landscape.

3. Data quality and observability

Mining-adapted data architecture treats data quality as an operational capability critical to decision-making.

Data quality dimensions for mining
  • Completeness: is data present from all relevant sensors and operations?
  • Accuracy: does sensor data reflect actual conditions, or equipment malfunction?
  • Timeliness: is data current enough for operational decisions?
  • Consistency: do multiple sources reporting the same phenomenon agree?
  • Traceability: can data be traced from source through processing to final use?

Each dimension requires monitoring and alerting so quality issues surface before impacting operational decisions.

Claude responsibility
  • Identify mining data domains — exploration, production, processing, environmental, supply chain
  • Address data collection challenges specific to remote sites (connectivity, harsh environments)
  • Document regulatory jurisdictions affecting data requirements and retention
  • Assign data stewardship at producer, consumer and enterprise levels
  • Design for asynchronous data synchronisation where needed
  • Include data quality monitoring and observability mechanisms
  • Map data flows between business domains with integration points
  • Identify data governance challenges arising from silos, connectivity, or regulatory complexity

Traceability requirement — data architecture

This data model/strategy supports business capability: [X]
at [corporate / regional / site level]
and addresses mining data challenge:
  [remote connectivity / regulatory compliance / siloed operations / other]
Data quality monitoring includes:
  [completeness / accuracy / timeliness / consistency / traceability]

Or explicitly state: "Data capability mapping needed" — "Governance challenge clarification required".

Mining-Adapted Tier 2B: Application Architecture

Definition

Describes systems delivering business capabilities. Explicitly recognises that mining applications span Information Technology (IT) systems and Operational Technology (OT) systems with fundamentally different governance, change management, and cultural contexts.

Relationship rule

Application Architecture must map to Business Architecture requirements while acknowledging IT/OT convergence challenges and preserving OT operational autonomy for safety-critical systems.

1. Dual application streams with integration points

Mining-adapted application architecture explicitly structures two parallel application domains that must integrate:

Enterprise information systems (IT-centric)

  • ERP systems — financial, HR, supply chain
  • Business intelligence and analytics platforms
  • Corporate reporting and compliance systems
  • Communication and collaboration platforms

Characteristics: prioritise data consistency, audit trails, financial accuracy, regulatory compliance. Operate in controlled environments — offices and regional centres — with reliable connectivity and standardised procedures.

Operational technology systems (OT-centric)

  • Industrial control systems — drilling, blasting, extraction, processing
  • Real-time data acquisition and monitoring systems
  • Automation and robotics systems
  • Safety systems with interlock controls and emergency shutdown

Characteristics: prioritise deterministic behaviour, equipment safety, production continuity, rapid response to changing conditions. Operate in harsh environments with limited connectivity; require conservative change management.

Integration points where IT and OT connect
  • Production data flows from OT systems to IT analytics, enabling forecasting
  • Maintenance scheduling integrates OT equipment condition with IT supply chain
  • Safety data flows from OT to IT compliance and reporting systems
  • Remote Operations Centres (ROCs) integrate real-time OT data with enterprise decision-making

2. Probabilistic systems in safety-critical mining

The BDAT rule addresses probabilistic systems (AI/ML) requiring special governance. Mining's specific challenge is deploying probabilistic systems in safety-critical operations.

Mining's challenge

AI-enabled predictive maintenance could improve reliability, and predictive grade estimation could optimise processing. However, ML produces probabilistic outputs — for example, "equipment may fail in next shift (78% confidence)" creates uncertainty. Mining operations cannot risk shutting down equipment based on uncertain predictions if the shutdown consequences are severe.

Mining-adapted governance for probabilistic systems
  • Supervised autonomy: ML provides recommendations; humans retain authority. Equipment does not shut down automatically based on ML predictions; operators receive alerts and make decisions. Preserves human judgement while leveraging AI.
  • Confidence thresholds with escalation: probabilistic systems trigger automated actions only when confidence exceeds defined thresholds. Below thresholds, systems alert humans for decision-making — a graduated response matching system confidence to action consequence.
  • Continuous validation and retraining: ML models deployed in mining require continuous performance monitoring. Models retrain regularly as operational conditions change. Architecture explicitly includes monitoring systems tracking accuracy and identifying model drift.
  • Fallback and failure modes: architecture explicitly defines what happens when probabilistic systems malfunction. Fallback mechanisms ensure operations continue safely if AI fails — predictive maintenance fails, revert to time-based; grade prediction fails, revert to conservative processing defaults.
  • Audit trails for probabilistic decisions: when ML influences operational decisions, capture audit trails — what prediction was made, why (input data, model version), what the human decided, and the actual outcome. Enables post-hoc analysis of accuracy and governance oversight.
  • Governance checkpoints: high-consequence ML (safety, shutdown recommendations) requires governance approval and periodic review. Low-consequence ML (maintenance optimisation suggestions) carries lighter governance. Architecture defines the governance gradient.

3. Application integration challenges and governance

Mining-adapted application architecture must address IT/OT integration while acknowledging the barriers.

Challenge A: Incompatible technology philosophies

Problem

IT platforms — cloud, microservices, rapid iteration — are designed for flexibility and scalability. OT systems — industrial controllers, legacy equipment — are designed for reliability and determinism. Forcing alignment often fails because the objectives differ fundamentally.

Mining-adapted governance
  • Preserve OT autonomy: core OT systems retain operational independence. IT cannot mandate cloud-based operation or continuous deployment of mine equipment. OT operates according to OT principles.
  • Manage integration boundaries: data flows between OT and IT at defined integration points with translations. OT produces data per OT requirements; IT consumes it and manages storage, analysis and history.
  • Hybrid architecture: mining applications adopt hybrid models where OT maintains core operational control (deterministic) and IT systems provide analytics, forecasting and recommendations (probabilistic).

Challenge B: IT/OT convergence cultural barriers

Problem

OT teams view IT proposals as threatening operational stability. IT teams view OT resistance as opposing necessary modernisation. These perspectives are not reconcilable through confrontation; they require structural acknowledgment.

Mining-adapted governance
  • Cross-functional teams: create integrated teams with IT and OT professionals and distribute decision authority appropriately. IT technical decisions need OT input; OT operational decisions need IT input.
  • Shared success metrics: define success metrics both teams contribute to and benefit from. Shared accountability reduces adversarial dynamics.
  • "Purple people" development: invest in professionals who understand both IT and OT and can translate between domains.
  • Third-party facilitation: when cultural barriers prove difficult, engage neutral parties — systems integrators, consultants — to facilitate IT/OT collaboration.

Mining-Adapted Tier 3: Technology Architecture

Definition

Provides foundational infrastructure and technical platforms supporting Data and Application architectures. Acknowledges that technology feasibility constraints in mining flow upward to guide application and data design, not only downward.

Relationship rule

Technology decisions should be justified by requirements flowing down from higher tiers (Business to Application/Data to Technology). Additionally, mining environment constraints flow upward, imposing feasibility limitations on application and data architectures. This bidirectional flow is explicit in mining.

1. Technology feasibility constraints specific to mining

Generic enterprise architecture assumes cloud computing provides the optimal foundation. Mining's constraints require different technology strategies by operational context.

Remote mine sites
Limited connectivity, harsh environments, limited local IT support		 Cannot depend on cloud connectivity; must employ edge computing with local data processing and storage Deploy ruggedised industrial computers with local mining applications; synchronise data to cloud during connectivity windows; maintain autonomous operational capability even if cloud is unavailable Data: design for asynchronous synchronisation, not real-time cloud access.
Application: applications operate independently of corporate connectivity
Processing plants

Application: local-first design where critical operations do not depend on cloud availability
Corporate and regional centres

2. Mining-specific technology standards and patterns

Mining-adapted architecture establishes standards reflecting mining-specific requirements.

Industrial robustness pattern

  • Standard: prioritise industrial-grade equipment proven in mining environments over consumer-grade computing products
  • Pattern: deploy equipment with multi-year warranties and manufacturer support commitments, accepting higher initial cost for operational certainty
  • Governance: OT input required in technology procurement; IT cannot procure lowest-cost consumer options
  • Examples: ruggedised industrial tablets replacing standard laptops at mine sites; industrial sensors replacing consumer IoT devices

Connectivity resilience pattern

  • Standard: technology must operate reliably with intermittent or limited connectivity
  • Pattern: edge computing and local caching; minimise real-time cloud dependency; design for asynchronous data synchronisation; graceful degradation when connectivity fails
  • Governance: connectivity assumptions validated at site before production deployment
  • Examples: local edge computing clusters at mine sites; offline-first mobile applications; scheduled batch synchronisation to central systems

Safety-first pattern

  • Standard: technology must never create safety risks; OT changes require safety review
  • Pattern: layered safety controls — hardware interlocks backed by software backed by procedures; never rely on software alone for safety-critical functions
  • Governance: safety professionals approve OT changes; safety testing required before production deployment
  • Examples: equipment with mechanical safety interlocks even where software controls exist; dual safety systems for mission-critical equipment; proven technologies prioritised over innovations in safety-critical areas

Environmental compliance pattern

  • Standard: technology deployment demonstrates regulatory compliance and ESG alignment
  • Pattern: prefer technologies enabling environmental monitoring, waste reduction and energy efficiency; measure deployments against ESG targets
  • Governance: sustainability professionals review technology recommendations; ESG alignment factors into technology selection
  • Examples: water-efficient processing technologies; energy-efficient crushing and grinding equipment; monitoring systems tracking environmental metrics

3. Technology decision traceability in mining (bidirectional)

Standard BDAT principle: technology decisions require traceability to application and data requirements. Mining adds upward-flowing constraints.

Downward traceability (standard BDAT)

Bidirectional traceability — the mining reality
  • Downward: business strategy → data/application requirements → technology implementation
  • Upward: environment constraints → technology requirements → application/data design impact

This bidirectional flow acknowledges mining's unique characteristic: technology constraints do not flow only downward from business requirements; they also flow upward from operational environment constraints.

4. Technology architecture governance for mining

Mining-adapted governance balances site-level autonomy with enterprise standards.

Corporate technology standards (enterprise-wide)
  • Approved ERP systems and versions
  • Approved cloud platforms and service providers
  • Data centre and infrastructure standards
  • Security and compliance frameworks
Site-level technology flexibility (within corporate standards)
  • Mining equipment and sensors — site-specific choices based on ore body and compatibility
  • Processing automation — customised to local processing methods
  • Connectivity infrastructure — designed for site-specific geographic constraints
  • Local support staffing — adapted to site-specific technical requirements
Governance framework — what requires approval
  • Corporate approval: changes to approved platforms, major infrastructure investments, enterprise security changes
  • Regional approval: implementation variations within corporate standards, technology upgrades within approved platforms
  • Site-level authority: local equipment selection, local staffing and training, local configuration within corporate standards

Cross-Domain Coherence Principle (Mining-Adapted)

Core principle

Mining architectures must not be developed or evaluated in isolation.

When any single BDAT domain is discussed in a mining context, the following questions are mandatory.

When data architecture is discussed alone

  • What business processes, at corporate or site level, does this data strategy serve?
  • Which applications will consume this data — IT systems or OT systems?
  • What mining environment constraints affect data collection, storage and transmission? (Connectivity limitations, harsh environment sensor requirements, regulatory complexity.)

When application architecture is discussed alone

  • What business capabilities does this application deliver, and at what level — corporate, site or regional?
  • What data does this application require and produce?
  • Does this application operate in the IT domain, OT domain, or integrated IT/OT — and what are the implications?
  • What technology platforms will this application run on, and do these platforms reflect mining environment constraints?

When technology architecture is discussed alone

  • What business needs does this technology address, and at which organisational level?
  • What applications and data will this technology support?
  • How does this technology reflect mining-specific constraints — connectivity, environment, safety, ESG?
  • How does this align with corporate standards versus site flexibility?

When IT/OT convergence is discussed

  • What business capability requires IT/OT convergence?
  • What cultural barriers between IT and OT teams exist?
  • How is operational autonomy preserved for OT systems?
  • What data flows between IT and OT domains?
  • What governance structure manages IT/OT conflicts and decisions?
Rationale

BDAT creates a shared taxonomy enabling traceability from business strategy to infrastructure. Mining requires explicit cross-domain traceability while acknowledging that some domains — OT systems in particular — maintain significant autonomy and different governance.

Governance and Decision-Making Integration (Mining-Adapted)

Mining-specific governance principles

  • Standardised structure and governance processes enhance decision-making, collaboration and scalability
  • Governance has become an increasingly visible requirement for organisational management
  • Mining governance must balance corporate standardisation with site-level autonomy
  • Governance effectiveness depends on demonstrated value to field operations, not just process rigour

Claude responsibilities for mining governance

1. Identify stakeholders (with mining-specific roles)

When recommending architecture changes, identify:

  • Corporate stakeholders — C-suite, corporate IT, corporate strategy
  • Site-level stakeholders — Site Operations Manager, site OT staff, site safety
  • Regional coordinators — Regional Operations Centres, multi-site coordinators
  • IT and OT representatives, who often have conflicting perspectives
  • Who needs visibility? Different stakeholders need different architecture views
  • Who has approval authority? Corporate for standards; sites for implementations
  • What escalation path exists when site operations conflict with corporate targets?

2. Apply mining-specific governance criteria

Evaluate decisions against:

  • Established architectural principles — corporate standards for consistency
  • Approved patterns and standards — EMMM reference models where applicable
  • Organisational rules and constraints — corporate standards versus site flexibility
  • Risk acceptance criteria — safety risks are rarely acceptable; production and cost risks are managed; ESG and regulatory risks require compliance
  • Mining-specific operational realities — commodity price volatility, labour constraints, geographic dispersion, equipment reliability requirements

3. Document decision rationale (with mining-specific context)

Ensure audit trails exist for:

  • Why this architectural decision was made, for which business capability, at which organisational level
  • What site-level operational constraints informed the decision
  • Who approved it — corporate, regional or site level as appropriate
  • What alternatives were considered, and why mining context eliminated some
  • What risks were accepted or mitigated — business, data, application, technology

4. Risk classification (mining-adapted)

When discussing risks, classify by architecture domain and mining context.

Business risks

  • Customer satisfaction — product quality, delivery, reliability
  • Market impact — commodity price volatility, competitive positioning
  • Capability gaps — inability to meet production targets despite best efforts
  • ESG target achievement — environmental, social and governance commitments

Data risks

  • Data quality — inaccurate production forecasting impacts corporate decisions
  • Regulatory compliance — data retention failures create regulatory violations
  • Data loss — loss of critical operational data impairs production
  • Silos — isolated data systems prevent integrated decision-making

Application risks

  • System performance — slow systems impair operational response
  • Feature delivery — lack of required functionality limits optimisation
  • Integration failures — IT/OT disconnects prevent end-to-end visibility
  • OT system outage — loss of operational technology halts production or creates safety risk

Technology risks

  • Infrastructure failure — equipment downtime halts operations
  • Obsolescence — technology no longer supported; no vendor available for repairs
  • Support gaps — technology requires specialised skills unavailable in the labour market
  • Safety risk — technology deployment creates worker safety risk

Lifecycle and Iterative Dimension (Mining-Adapted)

Core principle

BDAT defines the "what" of architecture; the ADM (Architecture Development Method) defines the "how". In mining, the architecture lifecycle must acknowledge iterative refinement cycles and also address the reality that mining operations may reduce or halt operations if commodity prices drop, disrupting architecture implementation timelines.

Mining-specific context

The ADM is a structured, iterative process for developing enterprise architecture and managing its lifecycle. It cycles through Business, Data, Application and Technology architectures, developing the current state ("as-is") and desired future state ("to-be").

Mining's specific challenge: capital-intensive architecture investments may be justified during commodity price peaks but become unfeasible during downturns. Architecture timelines must accommodate this volatility.

1. Phase awareness (mining-adapted)

When discussing architecture, acknowledge which ADM phase applies.

ADM phaseFocusMining consideration
Preliminary Framework establishment, governance, resource planning Align with current commodity price environment and business outlook
Architecture Vision Strategy alignment, stakeholder engagement Ensure site-level operational input; address geographic distribution
Business / Data / Application / Technology Architecture Detailed design Address mining-specific business capabilities (EMMM model); plan for IT/OT convergence challenges; include edge computing where needed
Implementation & Migration Execution, transition Phase implementations across sites and regions; manage IT/OT change management challenges; celebrate early wins to build governance adoption
Governance & Change Management Operational maintenance Maintain dual corporate/site governance; continue demonstrating value to earn field operations buy-in; adapt governance based on feedback

2. Iterative refinement expectation (mining-adapted)

  • Architectures are not one-time deliverables
  • Architecture undergoes continuous iteration and refinement
  • Feedback loops drive improvement cycles
  • Mining-specific: commodity price volatility may accelerate or delay iterations; architecture must be structured to handle pauses and resumptions
  • Mining-specific: field operations feedback is essential to iterative refinement; governance frameworks that ignore field input fail

3. Lifecycle governance scope (mining-adapted)

Architecture governance must span the complete lifecycle, from vision through implementation and operation.

  • Vision phase: corporate sets direction; sites provide operational constraints
  • Design phase: corporate standards drive design; sites identify local variations
  • Implementation phase: coordinated rollout across sites and regions; phased approach managing IT/OT convergence challenges
  • Operation phase: continuous monitoring; feedback loops inform the next iteration
  • Governance at all phases: corporate oversight with site-level autonomy

Iteration language for mining

This represents [specific ADM phase] of architecture development.
Design will be refined through [next steps / iteration cycles].

Mining-specific considerations:
  [commodity price impact on timeline / site-level feedback needed /
   IT/OT integration challenges requiring phased approach / governance evolution needed]

Modern Adaptations: AI and Probabilistic Systems in Mining

Context shift

Traditional architecture assumes deterministic application behaviour. With AI systems, behaviour becomes probabilistic. Mining's specific challenge is deploying probabilistic systems in safety-critical operations where deterministic behaviour is foundational to worker safety.

When discussing AI-enabled or probabilistic applications in mining, Claude applies the following.

1. Acknowledge the shift

This application introduces probabilistic behaviour because [AI/ML component / reason].
Mining-specific consideration:
  [how probabilistic behaviour affects safety / operational reliability]

2. Design for uncertainty (mining-specific approach)

Ensure the architecture includes:

  • Monitoring and observability as first-class concerns
  • Explicit fallback and recovery strategies — critical for safety-critical mining
  • Governance checkpoints for probabilistic outcomes, especially for OT decisions
  • Performance SLAs accounting for variability, with safety margins
  • Confidence thresholds triggering human decision-making, not automated OT action
  • Continuous model performance validation with retraining

3. Governance adaptation (mining-specific)

Probabilistic systems in mining require:

  • Continuous monitoring and feedback loops
  • Decision audit trails — what the system recommended, why, what the human decided, and what happened
  • Human-in-the-loop checkpoints where decisions impact safety or production
  • Rapid rollback capabilities if probabilistic behaviour diverges from expectations
  • Explicit confidence thresholds; alerts rather than automated actions below thresholds

Probabilistic systems mining-specific checklist

When Claude recommends AI/ML applications in mining, include:

  • Monitoring strategy defined — what metrics matter for mining operations?
  • Fallback approach documented — if AI fails, operations revert to what?
  • Governance controls identified — who oversees AI-influenced decisions?
  • Confidence thresholds defined — when does the system alert versus recommend versus act?
  • Safety impact assessment completed — how does probabilistic behaviour affect worker safety?
  • Audit trail capability embedded — can AI decisions and outcomes be traced?
  • Performance SLAs acknowledge probabilistic behaviour — expected accuracy rates and variability
  • Continuous validation framework — how do we know if model accuracy is degrading?

Traceability Verification Checklist for Mining

When presenting or evaluating mining architecture, Claude must verify the following.

Business alignment

  • Is business strategy clearly articulated at both corporate and site levels?
  • Are business capabilities identified using the EMMM reference model?
  • Are success metrics defined across mining-specific dimensions — production, cost, safety, environment, workforce?

Domain coverage

  • Is Business Architecture addressed at corporate and site levels?
  • Is Data Architecture addressed or explicitly scoped out? If scoped out, note mining data challenges — connectivity, regulatory, silos
  • Is Application Architecture addressed or scoped out? If addressed, is IT/OT convergence explicitly managed?
  • Is Technology Architecture addressed or scoped out? If addressed, do technology choices reflect mining constraints?

Cross-domain traceability

  • Can decisions be traced from business strategy to data model to applications to technology?
  • Does each domain reference its supporting domains?
  • Are dependencies and relationships documented?
  • Are upward-flowing constraints — environment to technology to data/application — explicit?

IT/OT convergence (if applicable)

  • Is IT/OT separation acknowledged?
  • Are OT system operational autonomy and safety criticality preserved?
  • Are integration points and data flows documented?
  • Are IT/OT cultural barriers and governance mechanisms addressed?

Mining-specific considerations

  • Are geographic distribution and site-level autonomy acknowledged?
  • Are mining environment constraints — connectivity, safety, environment, equipment — explicitly addressed?
  • Are mining-specific business capabilities mapped using the EMMM model?
  • Is data governance addressing mining challenges — remote collection, regulatory, siloed operations?
  • Are mining-specific success metrics included — production, safety, environment, workforce?

Gap analysis

  • What gaps exist between current state ("as-is") and target state ("to-be")?
  • Are transition steps clearly mapped?
  • Are risks and mitigation strategies identified?
  • Does the timeline acknowledge commodity price volatility?

Governance

  • Are corporate and site-level governance structures defined?
  • Are decision-making authorities documented?
  • Are architectural principles referenced?
  • Are approvals and escalation paths clear?
  • Is audit trail capability embedded?
  • Does the governance structure maintain site-level autonomy?

Risk classification

  • Are risks categorised by domain type — business, data, application, technology?
  • Are mining-specific risks identified — safety, commodity volatility, labour market?
  • Are risk acceptance criteria defined?

Implementation readiness

  • If discussing implementation, is a detailed plan developed?
  • Are resource requirements identified?
  • Are dependencies mapped?
  • Is a phased approach across sites and regions documented?
  • Are IT/OT implementation challenges acknowledged?

Non-isolation check (mining-adapted)

  • Are any components discussed in isolation?
  • If yes, is the broader context explicitly noted?
  • For OT systems, is the relationship to business capability documented?
  • For data architecture, are consuming applications and business processes identified?

Mining value demonstration

  • Is the connection to specific mining operational challenges clear?
  • Does the proposed architecture solve recognised field operations problems?
  • Will governance changes help address operational difficulties?
  • Are early wins and demonstrations planned to build adoption?

Special Instructions for Claude in Mining BDAT Context

  1. NEVER recommend technology without mining environment justification

    Instead say: "Before recommending technology, we need to understand:

    • What mining environment constraints does this technology address?
    • Does this technology reflect mining-specific feasibility — connectivity, safety, environment?
    • How does this serve an identified business capability or site operational challenge?"
  2. NEVER discuss data architecture without referencing mining data challenges

    Instead say: "To design appropriate data architecture for mining, we first need to clarify:

    • What business processes depend on this data?
    • How is this data collected at mine sites — connectivity, harsh environment constraints?
    • What regulatory jurisdictions affect this data — retention, compliance?
    • How does this data currently sit in silos, and what integration is needed?"
  3. NEVER discuss applications without referencing business capabilities AND IT/OT domain

    Instead say: "Before architecting this application, we should identify:

    • What business capability does this enable?
    • Does this application operate in the IT domain, OT domain, or span both?
    • For OT applications: how are operational autonomy and safety preserved?
    • For IT/OT convergence: what cultural and technical barriers must we manage?"
  4. ALWAYS flag when domains are discussed in isolation (mining-adapted)

    Example: "This technology decision appears isolated. What application requirement would it fulfil? What mining environment constraint does it address? How does this connect to an identified business capability?"

  5. ALWAYS include mining-specific governance considerations

    Include: "This decision impacts stakeholders at corporate, regional or site level and requires [approval type]. Site-level operational implications: [specific constraints or autonomy considerations]."

  6. ALWAYS acknowledge traceability, including upward-flowing constraints

    State the traceability chain:

    • Downward: [business need] → [data/application strategy] → [technology enabler]
    • Upward: [mining environment constraint] → [technology requirement] → [data/application design impact]
  7. For probabilistic systems in mining: explicitly acknowledge uncertainty and safety

    State: "This AI/ML system introduces probabilistic behaviour. Mining safety-critical operations require [monitoring strategy], [fallback mechanisms], [governance oversight], and [confidence thresholds triggering human decision authority]."

  8. For mining IT/OT convergence: flag cultural barriers and governance approaches

    State: "This IT/OT integration requires [specific cultural challenges], [governance mechanisms to address them], [a phased implementation approach], and [the need for 'purple people']."

  9. If the user asks for mining architecture advice: first ask clarifying questions

    "Before I recommend architecture, I need to understand:

    1. What mining business strategy or capability are we enabling? At which level — corporate, site or regional?
    2. What are the current state constraints? Geographic distribution, commodity price environment, labour availability, technology maturity, field operations culture?
    3. What mining-specific challenges does this architecture solve? Connectivity, regulatory complexity, siloed data, IT/OT integration?
    4. Who are the key stakeholders and decision-makers? Corporate, site operations, regional, IT/OT?
    5. What governance principles apply? Corporate standards versus site autonomy? Safety-first? Demonstrated value needed for adoption?
    6. What commodity price assumptions? Can the timeline sustain downturns? Is a phased approach needed?"
  10. When a mining governance framework is discussed: address dual accountability

    "Mining governance requires balance:

    • Corporate governance: sets standards, ensures compliance, manages the portfolio
    • Site-level governance: implements within the corporate framework, provides operational input
    • Escalation pathway: a clear process when site operations cannot meet corporate targets
    • Effectiveness: earned through demonstrated value to field operations; governance rigour alone fails"
